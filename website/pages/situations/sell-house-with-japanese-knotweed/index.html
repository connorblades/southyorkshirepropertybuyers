Is it legal to sell a house with Japanese knotweed?

Yes. No law stops you selling a house with Japanese knotweed. It isn't a crime to have it on your own land, and the sale registers at HM Land Registry like any other. The law controls four things: spreading it, getting rid of the waste, council enforcement, and what you tell the buyer. Each has clear rules you can follow.

Spread is regulated by the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981, Schedule 9. Section 14(2) makes it an offence to plant knotweed or cause it to grow in the wild. Having it on your own land isn't an offence. Letting it spread off your land into a neighbour's garden, a stream, a verge or an alley can be. Homeowners are rarely prosecuted. The focus is on developers and contractors moving contaminated soil.

Waste disposal is regulated under section 33 of the Environmental Protection Act 1990 and the Environment Agency's Regulatory Position Statement 178. Once you dig it up, knotweed and any soil within seven metres of it counts as controlled waste. It has to go to a licensed landfill through a registered waste carrier. Don't clear it yourself. Putting it in a skip or a garden bin turns a manageable garden problem into a legal one, and the fines are heavy.

Councils enforce through Community Protection Notices (CPNs) under sections 43-50 of the Anti-Social Behaviour, Crime and Policing Act 2014. You get a written warning first, then the notice, then a fixed penalty of up to £100, then prosecution. All four South Yorkshire councils have issued knotweed warnings, but only a handful each year. The best known prosecution is still Bristol City Council v MB Estate Limited (2018). A live CPN has to go on the TA6 form and shows up in the buyer's CON29 council search.

TA6 disclosure and Patarkatsishvili, what changed in 2025

TA6 question 7.8 is the most important piece of paper in a knotweed sale. It asks: "Is the property affected by Japanese knotweed? If yes, please state whether there is a Japanese knotweed management plan in place and supply a copy of any insurance cover relating to it." You can answer Yes, No or Not Known. The Law Society's notes say Not Known is the only safe answer if there is any doubt at all. Tick No when you aren't certain and you're the one exposed.

The case that changed this is Patarkatsishvili and Hunyak v Woodward-Fisher [2025] EWHC 265 (Ch). On 10 February 2025 the High Court unwound a completed London house sale. The seller's TA6 answers had played down a problem he knew about. It was clothes moths, not knotweed, but the same rules apply. Three findings carry straight across:

  • TA6 replies are statements of fact, not opinion.
  • A reply made without honest belief in its truth is fraudulent misrepresentation, not merely negligent.
  • Rescission, return of the property to the seller, is a real remedy, not a theoretical one.

Under section 32 of the Limitation Act 1980, the six-year clock runs from the day the buyer found out, not the day you sold. Knotweed that comes up three or four summers later can still land you in court. So if you have any reason to suspect knotweed, don't tick No. Tick Not Known, and there is no case against you.

The 2022 RICS Categories A, B, C and D in plain English

The RICS Japanese Knotweed and Residential Property Guidance Note (2022) replaced the old blanket seven-metre rule. Surveyors now sort knotweed into four categories, and the category is what drives everything else:

  • Category A: you can see it, and it's damaging the house, an outbuilding or a boundary wall. Digging it out is usually recommended. Most lenders refuse without a specialist excavation report. This is where the hit to your price is biggest.
  • Category B: it's within seven metres of the house and doing no damage, but it's blocking part of the garden. Herbicide plus an insurance backed guarantee is the usual path, and some lenders will still proceed.
  • Category C: it's there, but it isn't damaging anything or blocking anything. Treated as a minor issue. Lenders have relaxed a lot here since 2022, and the effect on price is usually small.
  • Category D: it isn't on your land at all, but it's within three metres of your boundary next door. It gets flagged, but lenders usually still lend once a specialist has looked at it.

Ask your surveyor to put the category in writing. It decides your route: Category A is rarely lendable without excavation, Category D usually is once a specialist has signed it off. Plenty of older reports still use the pre-2022 rules and make the problem sound worse than it is.

Mortgage lenders and the Insurance-Backed Guarantee

Most knotweed sales collapse because the buyer's lender says no at the valuation. Lenders want three things. First, a report from a Property Care Association or Invasive Non-Native Specialists Association member naming the plant and its RICS category. Second, a written treatment plan. Third, an Insurance-Backed Guarantee (IBG) that passes to the next owner.

The IBG is an insurance policy. It pays for more treatment, or compensates the owner, if the knotweed comes back and the original contractor can't put it right. It costs £300-£1,200, depending on how long the cover runs and how the work is done. Nationwide, Halifax, Santander, NatWest, Lloyds, Barclays, Yorkshire Building Society and the big mutuals all want one.

Where the main lenders stand at the moment:

  • Nationwide, lends subject to an IBG-backed 5-year treatment plan.
  • Halifax, lends with an IBG; shortened the required IBG window from 10 years to 5 years for some Category C cases in 2025.
  • Santander, now permits deferred-completion lending where treatment is in flight; requires treatment completion before drawdown for Category A and B.
  • NatWest, case-by-case; specialist report and IBG required.
  • Barclays, case-by-case with surveyor recommendation.
  • HSBC, declines Category A and B; considers C and D.
  • Yorkshire Building Society, specialist surveyor referral pathway rather than blanket refusal.
  • Skipton, Coventry, parts of the mutual sector, more cautious; periodic blanket refusals.

Lender rules change often, so check with a broker before you count on any one of them.

Selling a knotweed property in South Yorkshire, the local picture

Knotweed is far more common here than in most of the country. Sheffield is one of the worst affected cities in the UK, and Rotherham, Doncaster and Barnsley are all badly affected too. Three reasons: old industrial land from the furnaces and forges, former colliery ground, and the rivers and railway embankments that carry root fragments along with every flood. If a surveyor has found knotweed at your house, you are not unlucky and you are not alone.

The river corridors and the ten Sheffield hotspots

If you're near a river, you're more likely to have it. The Don, Sheaf, Loxley, Porter and Rivelin all carry knotweed. Floods tear root fragments from plants upstream and drop them in gardens further down. The Sheffield Star's mapping found ten clusters: Sharrow Vale Road and Hunters Bar; Heeley and Meersbrook; Nether Edge; Walkley and Crookes; Hillsborough and Owlerton; Wadsley Bridge and Wisewood; Tinsley, Brightside and Carbrook; Burngreave, Pitsmoor and Fir Vale; Stannington and Loxley; Stocksbridge. Every Sheffield postcode has confirmed cases, the city centre (S1, S2 west) is the only relatively low-risk zone.

Doncaster, Rotherham and Barnsley

Doncaster's knotweed pattern follows the Don, Dearne and Torne: Conisbrough to Doncaster town centre, Bentley and Arksey (flood-driven colonisation post-2007), Mexborough, Stainforth and Hatfield. Rotherham sits at the meeting of the Don and the Rother: Eastwood and Templeborough, Treeton, Maltby and Wickersley, Wath and Swinton. Barnsley's hotspots include Wombwell and Darfield, the Dearne valley (Goldthorpe, Thurnscoe, Bolton-on-Dearne), Penistone, Hoyland and the M1 J35-J36 verges.

Council reporting routes and empty-homes premium

Sheffield City Council takes reports through Streetscene; Doncaster via the "report a problem with grass cutting or planting" portal; Rotherham via the dedicated "Tell us about Japanese Knotweed on public land" form; Barnsley through general environmental services. All four treat their own land on rolling programmes. One thing does hit your pocket if the house is standing empty: council tax doubles once a property has been empty for 12 months, in all four boroughs, and climbs higher again in Sheffield after 5 and 10 years. On a sale that drags on for a year, that's real money.

When the knotweed is on a neighbour's land

A lot of South Yorkshire knotweed comes off public land. Network Rail's Sheffield, Rotherham, Doncaster, Wombwell and Penistone lines carry it. So do the Sheffield and Tinsley Canal and the Don Navigation, which belong to the Canal and River Trust, and the M1, A1(M) and A57 verges. If yours is spreading in from land like that, two court cases set out where you stand.

Network Rail Infrastructure Ltd v Williams and Waistell [2018] EWCA Civ 1514 decided that roots spreading onto your land is a private nuisance in itself. You can claim damages for loss of amenity, meaning the loss of the use and enjoyment of your own garden, even before anything is physically damaged. Since that ruling, Network Rail has tended to settle or treat rather than fight.

Davies v Bridgend County Borough Council [2024] UKSC 15 then made it harder to claim for the drop in your property's value. The Supreme Court held that the council's delay hadn't caused that loss, because the value had already fallen years earlier when the knotweed first came over. The amenity claim from Williams still stands. Selling doesn't kill off a live claim either, it usually passes with the land.

How to verify a legitimate cash buyer, the six-check playbook

The quick-house-sale sector isn't directly regulated. Oversight runs through The Property Ombudsman's Code of Practice for Residential Property Buying Companies, NAPB, AML Regulations 2017 (HMRC-supervised), and the Digital Markets, Competition and Consumers Act 2024. Knotweed sellers are systematically more exposed because the discount narrative is plausible and the information asymmetry is high. Run these six checks before signing anything.

  1. Companies House. Search the buyer's legal entity at find-and-update.company-information.service.gov.uk. Active status, filed accounts, real registered office, named directors.
  2. Proof of funds. Dated PDF bank statement (not a screenshot) on a named business account within 30 days, or a solicitor's undertaking that completion funds are held in client account. A buyer relying on bridging or a chain isn't a true cash buyer and must disclose that.
  3. TPO and NAPB membership. Verify on the live TPO member directory and NAPB directory. If a buyer claims membership but isn't listed, the claim is false.
  4. Reviews with depth. 30+ reviews spread over 12 months with identifiable detail. Read the one-stars. Real knotweed reviews mention specific markers: RICS category, IBG, lender refusal, contractor name, council CPN. Stock five-star reviews dated within a single week are a flag.
  5. Footer signals. Company number, registered office, ICO registration, complaints procedure. If those are missing, that tells you something. The most common complaint against quick-sale firms is a last-minute price cut blamed on a defect they already knew about, such as knotweed, damp or subsidence. A decent buyer will put it in writing that they won't renegotiate on the knotweed once it's been seen.
  6. Use your own solicitor, never the one the buyer picks. Your solicitor has to act for you and nobody else. You can check any solicitor is genuine on the Law Society's Find a Solicitor register.

A legitimate buyer welcomes all six checks. A buyer who pushes back on any of them is telling you something useful.

The honest gut-check, should you really sell to a cash buyer?

Three honest questions. We'd rather you walked away than took a route that doesn't fit you.

  1. Do you have a deadline within 12 weeks? A court date, a chain about to collapse, an estate to wind up, a lender chasing you. If you do, the open market's 22-40 weeks won't fit. If you don't, it can.
  2. Is the house costing you more than £600 a month? Count the mortgage, council tax, insurance, heating and upkeep. If it is, every extra month on the market eats into what you gain by waiting.
  3. Can you survive it falling through? Chains collapse, lenders pull out, surveys throw up new queries. Knotweed sales fall through more often than ordinary ones. Each time, you start again and lose the fees you've already paid.
