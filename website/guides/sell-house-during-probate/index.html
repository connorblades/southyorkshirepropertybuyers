Selling a house during probate: your options and next steps

When you are responsible for an estate, a property sale is one decision among many. This guide explains what to check, how to compare sale routes and what to tell us if you would like a direct offer.

Prepared by the South Yorkshire Property Buyers team. General information for property in England and Wales; ask the estate’s solicitor or tax adviser about your circumstances.

Can you sell a house during probate?

The answer depends on how the property was owned and who has authority to act. Probate gives the legal right to deal with an estate. A grant may be needed before an estate property can be sold, while property owned as joint tenants can pass to the surviving owner without probate. GOV.UK advises against marketing a property or making financial plans before probate is obtained. Read the official probate guidance and ask your solicitor to confirm the position before agreeing a sale.

You can contact us to explain the property and ask about our process. That conversation is not authority to sell, a binding sale agreement or a promise that completion can happen before the legal requirements are met.

How long will probate and the sale take?

GOV.UK’s current application guidance says grants or letters of administration usually arrive within 12 weeks of applying, with longer waits possible if more information is needed. That is not a timetable for valuing, administering and distributing the whole estate.

A property sale has its own legal work. The title, any mortgage or charges, the people entitled to act and the buyer’s checks all affect the date. Tell us whether the grant has been applied for or issued, whether a solicitor is instructed and whether there is a genuine deadline. We will discuss what looks achievable rather than promise a fixed completion date before those facts are known.

Would a direct offer help you plan?
Tell us about the property, the probate stage and the timing you have in mind. There is no obligation to accept an offer.

What should you gather before comparing offers?

You do not need to have every answer before speaking to us. These details help make the next conversation useful:

  • Authority and ownership: who is handling the estate, whether the grant is available and whether anyone else owns a share.
  • Condition and contents: known defects, any surveys, whether the home is occupied and what needs to remain or be removed.
  • Legal paperwork: the estate solicitor’s details and any title, lease, tenancy or mortgage information already available.
  • Timing: whether there is a date you need to work towards, or whether you want more time for family decisions.

Do not send us bank statements, beneficiary details or other sensitive estate papers through a general enquiry. Your solicitor can advise what evidence is needed and how to share it securely.

Compare the estate’s likely proceeds, not just headline prices

An estate agent, an auction and a direct purchase can involve different prices, costs and risks. Obtain written terms and ask the estate’s adviser to compare the expected proceeds after selling costs, mortgage repayment, any charges and applicable tax. Keep a record of valuations and offers, especially where other people have an interest in the estate.

An open-market sale may achieve a higher price if the estate can wait and the home is suitable for that route. A direct cash offer may be lower, in return for a simpler route without an onward buying chain. An offer remains subject to its written conditions and legal checks. No route should be described as guaranteed merely because a price has been discussed.

If considering auction, check the type of auction, reserve, fees, reservation terms and the point at which a sale contract becomes binding. Our auction and direct-sale comparison explains the questions to ask.

What about Inheritance Tax and Capital Gains Tax?

These are separate questions. Inheritance Tax concerns the estate and probate arrangements; Capital Gains Tax may arise if estate assets increase in value and are sold. The tax treatment can differ if the estate sells the property or a beneficiary inherits it and sells later. See GOV.UK’s guidance on selling estate assets.

Do not use an individual seller’s income-tax band to work out an estate’s CGT. HMRC’s current CGT guidance states that personal representatives pay 24% from 6 April 2026. Allowances, timing, valuations, deductions and reliefs need to be checked for the estate. Ask its tax adviser for the calculation before treating a gross offer as the amount available to distribute.

Does the house need clearing or repairing first?

You can ask about an offer before renovating or arranging viewings. Tell us about the condition and contents so that any proposed arrangements are clear. Keep personal possessions and important papers safe, and agree in writing what would be included in a purchase. A property needing work is assessed on its own facts; disclosure of known issues helps us explain an informed offer.

If the home is unsafe or access is restricted, tell us before arranging a visit. Do not enter an unsafe building to take photographs for an enquiry.

How would a direct purchase work?

Start with the property and the stage the estate has reached. We assess what you share, discuss any further checks and explain the proposed price and conditions. Your solicitor confirms the authority to sell and handles the conveyancing. A completion date is agreed around that legal work and the estate’s needs.

There is no charge to ask us for an offer and no obligation to accept. We charge no estate agent fees. We can cover legal fees if you use our panel solicitor; you may choose your own solicitor and pay their fees instead. Ask your solicitor about independence, costs and the written purchase terms before deciding.
