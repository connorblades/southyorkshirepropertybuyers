Yes, you can sell, and here are the two numbers no one separates

If a solicitor or agent has told you the property "can't be sold", what they almost always mean is that retail mortgage buyers can't buy until the defect is fixed. That is a statement about lender panel rules, the UK Finance Mortgage Lenders' Handbook Part 2, where each lender lists the title classes and unresolved defects they will accept, not a statement about the law. Under the Land Registration Act 2002, possessory, qualified and good leasehold titles all transfer cleanly. Cash buyers and specialist buyers transact on this stock every working day.

The reason many sellers end up with a poor outcome is that the industry has trained them to merge two separate numbers into one:

  • The property and its documents need to be assessed together. Share any survey, repair history, treatment plan, insurance information or title issue with the buyer and your solicitor. We consider properties with complications and explain our proposed terms after reviewing the details.
  • The indemnity insurance cost, what the defect actually adds. £20 to £300 for possessory title or absence of easement; £200 to £800 for an existing restrictive covenant breach; £1,000 to £2,000 for serious covenant breaches on higher value property. Stewart Title UK, CLS Property Insight and First Title Insurance underwrite these policies. The premium is paid once, binds successors in title, and is accepted by most mainstream lenders.

Ask the buyer to explain the offer conditions and ask your solicitor to identify any title work or insurance required. Keep proposed legal costs separate from the purchase price so you can understand what is being agreed.

Explain the title issue early

Tell your solicitor and any prospective buyer what you know about the title issue. Share the documents or correspondence you already have, explain any previous sale difficulty and mention the date you need to work towards. Your solicitor can identify what needs checking and what information a buyer will need.

The nine title defects we see most often

Nearly every enquiry sits in one of nine categories. Each has a routine fix, what differs is the timeline.

  1. Possessory title. Recognised class under section 9 LRA 2002. Upgrade to absolute after 12 years of unchallenged ownership on Form UT1. Sellable throughout. Indemnity £20 to £300.
  2. Unregistered land. Around 15% of land in England and Wales remains unregistered. First registration on Form FR1 with a good root of title; usual outcome is possessory title initially, upgradable later.
  3. Lost or destroyed deeds. For registered property the HMLR register is the proof, the solicitor orders an official copy. For unregistered property, reconstitution by statutory declaration plus first registration under Practice Guide 60.
  4. Restrictive covenant breach. Routine fix: indemnity in 24 to 48 hours (no contact with the beneficiary in the past 12 months). Slower fix: section 84 LPA 1925 application to the Upper Tribunal, 12 to 24 months.
  5. Missing or unrecorded easement. No documented right of way. Fix: deed of grant from the neighbour, prescriptive easement claim (20 years as of right), or absence of easement indemnity.
  6. Boundary dispute or discrepancy. Determined boundary application under rule 118 LRR 2003; or, post Brown v Ridley, adverse possession of the strip under Schedule 6.
  7. Adverse possession claims. Schedule 6 LRA 2002: window of 65 business days for a counter notice; three exceptions in paragraph 5; two year reapplication window in paragraph 6.
  8. Planning enforcement risk. From 25 April 2024 under the Levelling up and Regeneration Act 2023, England runs a single 10 year limit. After the period expires, a Lawful Development Certificate under section 191 TCPA 1990 regularises the position.
  9. Registered charge or restriction. Old mortgage discharge never registered; Form A trust restriction; HMRC notice. Most routine paperwork. Form DS1 or removal under Form RX3.

Is it legal to sell? The state guarantee and what changed in 2025 to 2026

The Land Registration Act 2002 sections 9 to 11 set out four classes of registered title: absolute (full state guarantee), good leasehold, possessory, and qualified. All four can be sold. All four transfer cleanly on completion. The lender pool narrows as you move from absolute towards qualified; the law itself doesn't change. Four 2025 to 2026 developments materially shifted the landscape and most of the top ranking national pages haven't integrated them:

  • Brown v Ridley [2025] UKSC 7, the Supreme Court clarified that the 10 year "reasonable belief in ownership" period required under the boundary route to adverse possession (paragraph 5(4)(c) of Schedule 6 LRA 2002) doesn't have to be the 10 years immediately before the application. Any continuous 10 year period within the adverse possession suffices. Widens the route to register disputed boundary strips around Penistone, Wickersley, Maltby, Wath and the colliery villages. Nazir v Begum [2025] EWCA Civ 587 then refined the Schedule 6 procedure on competing applications. 2025 has been the most claimant friendly year for adverse possession since the LRA 2002 came into force.
  • TA6 Property Information Form 6th edition, mandatory for Conveyancing Quality Scheme firms from 30 March 2026. Sellers must now disclose any past or present neighbour dispute, alterations to boundary features, and known title issues. The disclosure landscape for boundary defects and historic informal arrangements is materially wider than under the 5th edition.
  • Patarkatsishvili v Woodward-Fisher [2025] EWHC 265 (Ch), on 10 February 2025, Mr Justice Fancourt rescinded a £32.5m Notting Hill sale for fraudulent misrepresentation in TA6 replies. A TA6 reply made without honest belief in its truth is fraudulent misrepresentation; rescission is a real remedy; and under section 32 of the Limitation Act 1980 the six year clock for fraud runs from discovery, not contract.
  • Digital Markets, Competition and Consumers Act 2024, in force April 2025. Sellers and any business acting for them must disclose all material information, including known title defects. The Competition and Markets Authority has direct enforcement powers with civil penalties up to £300,000 for individuals or 10% of global turnover for businesses. A legitimate buyer will insist on full disclosure for exactly that reason.

Indemnity insurance, what it actually costs

Indemnity insurance is the workhorse of title defect sales. A one off premium buys a policy that protects the buyer (and successors in title and their mortgagee) against the financial consequences of the named defect crystallising. Once issued, the policy runs with the property in perpetuity. Once the covenant beneficiary, neighbour, council or church has been put on notice, the risk has materialised and the policy is no longer available. A seller who has said nothing about a long dormant defect is in a stronger position to buy indemnity than one who has tried to negotiate.

The three routes: fix first, cash buyer, or auction

Once you have an honest view of which defect you have and which lender pool will accept it, the choice of route is mechanical. The three routes differ on timeline, headline price, and the cost of getting there.

Your own SRA regulated solicitor, essential

This is the most important practical step a title defect seller can take. The seller's own solicitor: separately instructed, separately paid, separately answerable to the seller, is the only person in the transaction whose duty runs only to the seller. Any buyer who pushes the seller towards the buyer's nominated firm has failed the test. The honest practical check, should you really sell to a cash buyer?

Run three tests honestly. We would rather you walked away than took a route that doesn't fit.

  1. Your deadline. Ask your solicitor which title issues must be resolved before a sale and what timetable is realistic. Compare that with the date you need to work towards.
  2. Compare the written terms for your property, including fees, ongoing costs, buyer funding and the proposed completion date. We assess each property individually. Your solicitor can explain the conditions, mortgage redemption and any other deductions before you decide.
  3. Sale risks. Ask the buyer and your solicitor about funding, chain dependencies, known title issues and any surveys or documents still needed. Understand what could delay or prevent completion.

Compare each route using written offers, the work still needed, costs and a realistic completion date. Ask your solicitor to explain how the title issue affects your options before you commit.
