Repossession stats South Yorkshire 2026, how to read the MoJ numbers

Every quarter the Ministry of Justice publishes the numbers on possession claims across England and Wales. The headlines make the news; the local-authority tables underneath usually don't. This guide explains what the MoJ tables actually measure, how mortgage claims differ from landlord claims, how to look up Sheffield, Rotherham, Doncaster and Barnsley for yourself, and what the direction of travel through 2025 and into 2026 means for a borrower in arrears.

Where the numbers actually come from

There are two distinct data sources that get called "repossession statistics" in headlines, and they measure different things.

The first is the Ministry of Justice's Mortgage and Landlord Possession Statistics, published quarterly, which counts what happens in court: claims issued, orders made, warrants applied for, and repossessions enforced by County Court Bailiff. It is drawn from the HMCTS case management system and is the definitive record of possession activity in England and Wales. It also splits neatly into mortgage possession (lender vs borrower) and landlord possession (landlord vs tenant).

The second is UK Finance's Household Finance Review, which counts mortgage arrears from the lender side: how many borrowers are behind by 2.5% or more of the outstanding balance, split by loan-to-value band and product type. It is a leading indicator of where court volumes will be a year later, because arrears normally precede claims.

Both are useful. If you want to understand the local picture, MoJ is the one to open; UK Finance tells you the national temperature but does not break down to Sheffield or Doncaster.

Mortgage claims vs landlord claims, why the distinction matters

A mortgage possession claim is brought by a lender against a borrower who has fallen into arrears on a residential mortgage. The lender's goal is a court order allowing them to take the property back and sell it to recover the debt. The controlling rules are the Pre-Action Protocol for Possession Claims based on Mortgage Arrears, the FCA's MCOB 13 forbearance rules, and the court's discretionary powers under section 36 of the Administration of Justice Act 1970.

A landlord possession claim is brought by a landlord against a tenant. For 2026 that means either a Section 8 claim under the Housing Act 1988, which is now the primary route since Section 21 was abolished under the Renters Rights Act, or the accelerated procedure for older tenancies that still have a Section 21 notice served under the transitional rules. Landlord claims have their own statutory grounds; some are mandatory, some are discretionary.

Both sets sit in the same MoJ release, but they behave very differently. Mortgage volumes track interest rates, unemployment and the wider household budget picture with a lag. Landlord volumes track legislative change (like the 2026 Renters Rights Act commencement), the availability of alternative rented accommodation, and the level of rent arrears at scale. Do not conflate them in your head when reading the headline number.

How to look up Sheffield, Rotherham, Doncaster or Barnsley

The MoJ release is not the easiest document to navigate on a first visit. Here is the step-by-step:

  1. Go to the Mortgage and Landlord Possession Statistics collection page on GOV.UK.
  2. Open the most recent quarterly release, titled "Mortgage and Landlord Possession Statistics: [month] to [month] [year]".
  3. Scroll down to the "Documents" section and find the local authority tables. They are normally published as an ODS (OpenDocument spreadsheet) file, with one worksheet per metric (claims, orders, warrants, repossessions) and per procedure type (mortgage, landlord, and within landlord: social, private and accelerated).
  4. Filter or search for the local authority name: Sheffield, Rotherham, Doncaster or Barnsley.
  5. Read across the row for the quarter you care about. The rate is expressed per 100,000 households so that boroughs of different sizes can be compared directly.

If spreadsheets are not your thing, the release also includes a set of pre-rendered summary tables and a commentary PDF. The tables have the numbers; the commentary has the interpretation. For most homeowners a two-minute look at the summary tables is enough to answer the question "am I in a rising or falling area".

What the trend has looked like through 2025 and into 2026

The direction of travel across the recent MoJ quarterly releases has been consistent. Mortgage possession claims, orders, warrants and repossessions have all risen from their pandemic-era lows. The trigger is straightforward: the two-year and five-year fixed-rate mortgages taken out at 1.5%-2.5% in 2020-2022 rolled off onto rates in the 4%-6% range as they matured through 2023, 2024 and into 2025. Payment shocks of £300-£600 a month on a typical South Yorkshire mortgage do not always end in arrears, but they end in arrears often enough for the court numbers to move.

Volumes remain well below the 2008-2013 peak. That peak was driven by a very different combination: a banking crisis, widespread negative equity, and lender behaviour that had not yet been shaped by the Mortgage Charter, the post-crisis MCOB rules, or the current pre-action protocol. The 2026 picture is one of steady, cyclical pressure, not systemic collapse.

Landlord claims moved faster than mortgage claims over the same period. Rent arrears built up during and after the cost-of-living squeeze; the Renters Rights Act 2026 changed the shape of the possession routes available; and the mix of Section 8 grounds being used shifted materially. The 2026 quarterly releases break landlord out into social, private-Section 8, and accelerated categories, and the mix tells you as much as the totals do.

The South Yorkshire picture in context

Post-industrial urban local authorities in the North of England and the Midlands have historically sat above the England and Wales average on landlord possession claims per 100,000 households, and around or slightly above average on mortgage claims. That pattern reflects the local mix of housing tenure (a higher share of private rented and social rented stock than the England average), income distribution, and stock condition, not a single "bad borough" effect.

Within South Yorkshire, all four boroughs share a broadly similar structural picture, but with real differences in the detail:

  • Sheffield has the largest household count of the four, a large private rented sector concentrated in S2, S4, S6 and S10, and a large student population that shapes tenancy churn. Landlord claims are a bigger share of the total than in the smaller boroughs.
  • Rotherham has a lower average household income than Sheffield and a higher share of ex-industrial owner-occupied terraces. Mortgage claim rates have historically been closer to the England average than to Sheffield's.
  • Doncaster has a spread-out geography with distinct sub-markets (town centre, Bentley/Rossington, Thorne, Tickhill), a substantial private rented sector, and pockets of higher-than-average landlord claim activity in the DN1-DN4 postcodes.
  • Barnsley is the smallest of the four by household count and has a housing mix skewed towards owner-occupation of former colliery-village stock. Mortgage claims are a proportionally larger share of the borough's total than in the other three.

None of that is deterministic. Individual outcomes depend far more on your personal financial position and your lender's behaviour than on your borough.

What the funnel looks like: claims, orders, warrants, repossessions

The single most important thing to understand about the MoJ tables is that they show a funnel. Each stage produces fewer cases than the stage before:

Stage What it means Why it drops
Claim issued Lender or landlord files N5 at County Court. Baseline volume; every subsequent stage is a subset.
Order made District Judge grants possession, often suspended on terms. Some claims settle, are withdrawn, or are adjourned indefinitely.
Warrant issued Lender applies for a bailiff appointment after the order date passes without compliance. Many borrowers cure the arrears, sell voluntarily, or the order is complied with.
Repossession enforced County Court Bailiff attends and takes possession. Warrant suspensions on the day, or last-minute completions on a sale, remove cases.

Source: structure of the MoJ Mortgage and Landlord Possession Statistics quarterly tables. Exact stage-by-stage attrition varies by quarter and by borough.

The reason this matters: a headline that says "claims rose 20% in South Yorkshire" is not the same as "repossessions rose 20% in South Yorkshire". At any point in the funnel a borrower can pay off the arrears, agree a suspended order on Norgan terms, refinance, or sell the property voluntarily. The MoJ tables let you see the drop-off at every step, and it is substantial.

What this means for you if you are behind on the mortgage

Two practical takeaways.

First, the statistics do not decide your case. If a claim is issued against you, the outcome is shaped by your written proposal, your income evidence, your lender's compliance with the Pre-Action Protocol, and whether you can show the court there is a realistic path back to sustained payments (or a realistic buyer in place). The Cheltenham & Gloucester v Norgan principle, that the "reasonable period" for repaying arrears should normally be measured against the remaining mortgage term, gives most borrowers a route to a suspended order if they can show an affordable proposal.

Second, if the arithmetic no longer supports staying, a voluntary sale beats an enforced sale on almost every measure that matters: more money in your pocket, less damage to your credit file, no bailiff record, and the ability to plan the move on your own timetable. The MoJ funnel confirms that most claims never reach enforced repossession precisely because most borrowers act before the bailiff date. A cash sale is the fastest voluntary route, typically 2-3 weeks from written offer to completion, and it is the one a District Judge can most easily accept as evidence of a resolution when considering whether to suspend possession.
