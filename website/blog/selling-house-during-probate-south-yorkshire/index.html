Probate house sale timeline: the stages and checks

Sorting out a home after a bereavement can mean paperwork, belongings and family decisions arriving together. Separating the estate process from the property sale helps you see what can be prepared and what still needs to happen.

Published 1 April 2026. Updated 10 November 2026.

Quick answer: A probate house sale has several stages, rather than one reliable countdown. First establish the ownership and authority to act. Then track the estate application, property documents, buyer readiness and legal checks separately. A grant arriving does not by itself mean the sale is ready to complete. Ask the estate solicitor what remains before relying on a moving or distribution date.

1. Establish who can deal with the property

Start with the will, the ownership documents and the people dealing with the estate. A property in the deceased person's sole name needs different handling from a home with a surviving joint owner. Do not assume that being a beneficiary means you can instruct a sale yourself.

The government's probate guidance explains who can apply and when probate may be needed. Ask the estate solicitor to confirm the appropriate grant, who must act and any title restrictions. Our article on selling before probate is granted explains why a simple yes or no can miss the legal position.

2. Gather the documents and look after the home

While the legal position is being confirmed, getting the information together can make later conversations more useful. You do not need to clear every room or organise a renovation just to explain the situation.

  • Keep the will, grant application correspondence and details of the people authorised to act together.
  • Locate the title information, mortgage correspondence and any existing property agreements.
  • Record known defects, surveys, notices, tenancy arrangements and access limitations.
  • Check insurance conditions, security and who will deal with urgent maintenance.
  • Keep a record of bills and ask the relevant providers about the estate's actual obligations.

Ask the estate adviser which tax reporting or payment matters need attention. Avoid building a sale timetable around an assumed exemption or a general online estimate.

3. Track the estate application without promising a date

The application may need information from several people or organisations. Ask who is responsible for each outstanding item and keep copies of requests and replies. A general processing average cannot tell you when this particular estate will be ready.

GOV.UK explains what happens after a probate application. Use the current official guidance and the estate solicitor's update when checking progress. GOV.UK also advises waiting until probate before putting a property on the market or making financial plans; ask your solicitor how that guidance applies to the ownership and estate you are dealing with.

4. Check what remains when the grant arrives

Where a grant is required, receiving it addresses an important part of the estate process. It does not finish the buyer's checks, resolve a title issue or make an empty house ready for safe access.

Ask the conveyancer to confirm which documents are accepted, which enquiries remain, whether the buyer's funds are ready and what has to happen before exchange. Keep the proposed completion date separate from the point at which contracts become binding. A discussion about a date is not the same as a completed transaction.

5. Choose a route around the estate and the property

An estate agent, auction or direct purchase may involve different preparation, terms and buyers. Compare the written conditions, charges and work remaining, with the estate's solicitor advising on the decision. If several people need to review the options, keep the same documents available to everyone involved.

A cash buyer removes that buyer's mortgage dependency. It does not remove the estate's legal requirements or guarantee completion immediately after a grant.
