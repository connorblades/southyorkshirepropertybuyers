What to do when your house sale falls through

If your buyer has just pulled out, or your chain has collapsed, this guide covers exactly what happens next. What it costs you, whether you can claim anything back, and your realistic options for moving forward.

Published 22 April 2026

Quick answer: Ask your agent and solicitor why the sale stopped, what costs or commitments remain and whether another buyer is ready to proceed. You can compare relisting, changing agents, auction and a direct cash offer. A buyer who does not need a mortgage or an onward sale can remove those dependencies, but property and legal checks still matter. Agree a realistic timetable before relying on a new moving date.

The reality of a sale falling through

When a sale collapses, it isn't just the loss of one transaction. It is the months you put into it. The viewings. The offer negotiations. The solicitor correspondence. The plans built around a moving date that no longer exists.

That is genuinely awful. And it is worth saying that clearly before anything else.

A failed sale can leave you repeating work while an onward purchase or moving plan waits. If you want to compare another route, our page on selling when your sale has fallen through explains a direct purchase. Understanding why the previous buyer withdrew helps you decide what to change before starting again.

For a more detailed checklist, read what to check after a house sale falls through. It covers the remaining legal work, the buyer's funding and the questions to ask before agreeing a new timetable.

Why do house sales fall through?

Understanding what happened can help you avoid it next time and give you a clearer sense of which route forward makes sense.

The most common reasons a sale collapses before exchange:

The buyer's mortgage offer was withdrawn. Lenders can revise or withdraw offers if the buyer's circumstances change, if the survey raises concerns about the property, or if the lender's own criteria shift.

The survey raised problems. A survey identifying structural issues, damp, subsidence or Japanese knotweed may lead a buyer to seek further advice, renegotiate or withdraw. Find out what the report actually says before deciding whether repairs or a different selling route make sense.

The chain fell apart. Your buyer may be unable to proceed because their own sale stopped. A buyer who does not need to sell another property removes that particular dependency, but does not eliminate every risk to the transaction.

The buyer changed their mind. An ordinary offer is not binding in England and Wales until exchange of contracts. Separate agreements may affect the costs or obligations if someone withdraws. Ask your solicitor to check what has been signed.

The delays wore them down. A conveyancing process that stretches to five or six months creates a long window for life to change: jobs, relationships, finances, priorities.

What a fallen-through sale actually costs you

This is usually the first question people have. How much has this set you back?

The answer depends on how far the sale had progressed.

Your solicitor's fees. Your solicitor charges for the work completed up to the point the sale collapsed. If it fell through early, before much work was done, the cost may be minimal. If searches were completed and enquiries were well under way, expect to pay somewhere between £500 and £1,000 for an abortive file. Ask your solicitor for a clear breakdown.

Your Energy Performance Certificate. If you arranged an EPC, that cost stands, typically £60 to £120. An EPC remains valid for ten years, so it carries forward to your next sale. It is still a sunk cost, but not a wasted one.

The buyer's survey. This is the buyer's own cost. They will have lost their survey fee too, but that isn't your liability.

Estate agent fees. If your agent operates on a no-sale-no-fee basis, you will owe nothing. If you have a fixed-fee upfront arrangement, check the terms you signed at the start.

Please note: taxes including Capital Gains Tax aren't covered by any cash buying service and remain your own responsibility. We recommend seeking independent tax advice if applicable.

Can you claim anything back from the buyer?

An ordinary offer is not binding in England and Wales until exchange. A buyer who withdraws before then will not usually reimburse your costs simply because the sale was agreed. Separate reservation, exclusivity or agency terms may create obligations or charges before exchange. Ask your solicitor to check anything you signed and any costs already incurred.

The government's guidance on offers and negotiations explains when a property sale becomes binding. Its guide to selling a home also explains that a reservation agreement may include a financial penalty for withdrawing.

Do not assume a deposit is automatically yours to keep or that every withdrawal has the same consequence. The written terms, the reason for withdrawal and the legal position need to be checked. A separate agreement does not guarantee that a sale will complete.

When the buyer keeps stalling but hasn't formally pulled out

Some sales don't collapse cleanly. They just go quiet. Emails take longer to return. Solicitor requests go unanswered. Weeks pass and you aren't sure whether the sale is still happening.

Signs that a sale is in serious trouble:

  • The buyer has stopped responding to their own solicitor
  • Searches have expired and no one has agreed to refresh them
  • The buyer's mortgage offer has lapsed with no update on renewal
  • Their own sale fell through and they haven't confirmed they still intend to proceed
  • Communication has dropped without explanation for several weeks

What to do: Ask your solicitor and agent to confirm the current position, what remains outstanding and whether the buyer intends to proceed. Agree a suitable response deadline with them. Before relisting, check any reservation, exclusivity or agency commitments and whether the existing sale has formally ended.

A direct conversation, if you have the buyer's contact details, often resolves uncertainty faster than solicitor correspondence. Not every silence is a withdrawal. Sometimes buyers are dealing with something on their end and need a direct prompt.

When a sale has fallen through more than once

If this has happened on the same property twice or more, it is worth taking an honest look before relisting in exactly the same way.

The asking price. Is it based on what similar properties are actually completing at, rather than listing prices? If buyers are consistently reaching a certain stage and then withdrawing, the price may be a factor, particularly if their survey is coming in below the agreed figure.

Known survey issues. If buyers' surveys are flagging the same problem repeatedly, that issue either needs to be addressed before relisting or clearly reflected in the price from the start. Ambiguity creates withdrawal.

The listing itself. A property that has been on the market for months with unchanged photographs and the same description looks stale. Refreshing the listing: new photos, updated copy, sometimes a modest price adjustment, changes how buyers approach it.

Your realistic options from here

Relist with your current agent

If the relationship is solid and the agent has a strong local pipeline, this is often the quickest route back to market. Ask them directly: are there any second-placed buyers who are still available? What would they do differently this time?

Switch to a different agent

If you have lost confidence in how the sale was handled, a fresh agent brings a different buyer pool and a different approach to marketing. Sometimes a change of presentation alone makes a significant difference to the quality of interest.

Property auction

With a traditional unconditional auction, exchange usually takes place when the hammer falls. With a modern auction, read the reservation agreement carefully. Ask the auctioneer to explain the fees, reserve, completion terms and what happens if the property does not sell.

Cash buyer

A direct cash buyer removes the need for that buyer to sell another property or obtain a mortgage. A sale can still be affected by legal checks, property issues or a change before exchange. Ask your solicitor when it becomes binding and agree a realistic timetable around the remaining work.
